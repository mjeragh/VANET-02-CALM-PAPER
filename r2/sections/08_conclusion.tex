\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to revise a paper that claimed an imitation-anchored MARL method produced diverse, human-like and safe cooperative driving, and found that the pipeline behind it could not have shown any of the three. A prior and an actor that read different features, an action order never mapped, agents dropped before they departed, a simulator that changed lanes for the policy, an in-sample comparison without a majority baseline and an entropy that was not the defined one each moved the reported numbers towards the conclusion we had drawn, and together they presented a policy that always commands the same action as a success.

Rebuilt under one recorded contract and evaluated with decision rules fixed in advance, the study supports three findings. Fine-tuning a behaviour-cloned prior with MADDPG and no anchor collapses it into a low-entropy lane oscillator on both seeds. A KL anchor to the prior prevents that collapse, whatever the training action-selection rule. And the anchored policy is no better than the frozen prior it was initialised from on any declared measure under the execution rule it trains with. It also establishes three identities that stand as checks for similar studies: the greedy anchored policy is a constant policy, zero collisions is the simulator's property, and no method reaches the majority class on agreement with the human labels.

The method that survives is therefore a modest one, a regulariser that keeps a cloned policy intact through reinforcement learning, and its usefulness depends on there being something for the reinforcement learning to add, which this benchmark does not offer. The next step for this line of work is not a larger experiment on the same scenario but a different scenario: one in which the exit policy is not a constant, the agents' goals conflict so that coordination can be measured, safety is not guaranteed by the simulator's driver model, and the human reference describes the traffic state the task poses. Until then, the contribution we can stand behind is the defect catalogue, the contract and the checklist, offered so that the errors we made are cheaper for others to find than they were for us.
