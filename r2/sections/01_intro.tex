\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In a manuscript we previously submitted, and have since withdrawn, we reported a method called CALM for cooperative highway-exit coordination among connected automated vehicles~\cite{guanetti2018control,wang2021cooperative}. CALM clones a prior from NGSIM Interstate~80 trajectories~\cite{ngsim2006}, copies it into the actors of a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) algorithm~\cite{lowe2017multi,rashid2018qmix}, and fine-tunes under a KL penalty towards the frozen prior whose weight decays over training. The manuscript claimed that unconstrained MARL in this task suffers mode collapse, that entropy regularisation does not resolve it, that CALM prevents it, and that the resulting policies are human-like (validated against a held-out NGSIM set), reach the highest reward among diversity-preserving methods, and have the best safety profile in the SUMO simulator~\cite{lopez2018microscopic}, with zero collisions.

While preparing a revision we audited the pipeline that produced those numbers and found that most of them did not measure what the text said they measured. The prior had been fitted to four normalised NGSIM features while the actor it was copied into received raw simulator quantities, so the KL anchor compared distributions over different inputs; the prior's action order was never mapped to the simulator's, so its ``Maintain'' executed as a lane change; the safety evaluator discarded four of the five agents before they had departed, which turned a 100\,\% exit rate into 33\,\%; SUMO's own lane-change model, not the policy, moved the agents into the exit lane; the NGSIM comparison was in-sample; no majority-class baseline stood beside the agreement figures; and the reported ``entropy'' was not the quantity the paper's own definition named. Each defect is small, none is visible from the reported numbers, and together they made a degenerate policy---one that always commands the same action---look diverse, safe and human-like.

We could have withdrawn the manuscript and stopped. We did not, for two reasons. First, the defects are not specific to our code: every one of them is a natural thing to do when an imitation-learning prior trained on recorded data is combined with reinforcement learning in a traffic simulator and scored against the recording, and several of them are invisible unless one asks questions a results table never prompts (who moved the vehicle, how many agents were counted, what would a constant policy score). Second, once the pipeline was rebuilt under a single declared contract, the original question could be answered, and the answer is worth having even though it is not the one we had claimed.

Under the corrected contract three things are measurable in this benchmark, and we measured them with decision rules written before the runs. Fine-tuning the cloned prior with MADDPG and no anchor destroys it within 300 episodes: per-state action entropy falls from 0.78 to 0.22--0.25 over simulator rollouts and the policy becomes a lane oscillator that exits in 38--45\,\% of cases. With the anchor the same prior, fine-tuned from identical weights under identical conditions, keeps entropy 0.71 in the simulator and 0.81 on held-out human states, on both seeds, and the pre-registered rule for the wording ``anchoring prevents collapse'' passes. But the anchored policy is no better than the frozen prior it protects on any declared measure: on the same episodes the prior has equal-or-higher entropy, more exits (99\,\% against 80--85\,\%) and the same speed. Fine-tuning trained out the prior's lane oscillation and gained nothing else we could measure. Alongside these, three identities hold that any similar study should check: the anchored policy evaluated greedily is the constant Lane\_Right policy; zero collisions is a property of SUMO's car-following model for every policy, stranded and oscillating ones included; and NGSIM action agreement is below the 55\,\% majority-class baseline for every method, ours and the comparators'. The anchor is a regulariser that preserves, not one that improves, and the benchmark cannot test human-likeness, safety or cooperation at all.

This paper is therefore a methods-and-evaluation paper built on the correction of our own study. Its contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A defect catalogue for an imitation-plus-RL driving pipeline---three input/action-contract defects, two evaluator defects, an in-sample comparison and a missing baseline---each with its mechanism and its measured effect on the numbers we had reported (Section~\ref{sec:defects}).
  \item A corrected and recorded evaluation contract for such pipelines: one observation space and action order from prior to evaluator, a vehicle-grouped data split with a frozen test set, three named entropy quantities and a declared selection rule, a declared execution rule, policy-attributable lane changes, exposure-normalised proximity, constant-action and simulator-default reference policies, matched episodes and paired inference (Section~\ref{sec:contract}).
  \item Three identity results that any such study must check: greedy evaluation of the anchored policy equals the constant Lane\_Right policy; zero collisions equals SUMO's car-following, for every policy; NGSIM action agreement below the majority baseline for every method (Section~\ref{sec:concealed}).
  \item A pre-registered isolation of the anchoring effect: fixed initialisation, one training and evaluation contract, matched episode seeds, a decision rule written before the runs (Section~\ref{sec:anchor}), and the frozen-prior control on the same matched episodes (Section~\ref{sec:prior}).
  \item A testability checklist for the MultiExit\,+\,NGSIM benchmark and a reporting checklist for SUMO\,+\,NGSIM evaluations of imitation-anchored RL (Sections~\ref{sec:testability} and~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

Two rules govern what follows. Every number in Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:testability} was produced under the contract of Section~\ref{sec:contract} and comes from a recorded run; nothing measured on the submitted pipeline is used as a comparator, and the submitted tables appear only as a historical record, labelled as measurements of the defective pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:concealed} and the appendix). And the methods we had compared against---DAgger~\cite{ross2011reduction}, GAIL~\cite{ho2016generative}, a soft actor--critic variant~\cite{haarnoja2018soft} and an entropy-coefficient sweep---were not re-run under the corrected contract and are not ranked against CALM here; the only comparisons this paper makes are between the anchored policy, the identically initialised unanchored policy, the frozen prior and the reference policies that learn nothing. Section~\ref{sec:background} recalls the method and places it among KL-regularised fine-tuning and evaluation-methodology work; Section~\ref{sec:defects} gives the defects; Section~\ref{sec:contract} the contract; Section~\ref{sec:results} the results; Section~\ref{sec:testability} what the benchmark can test; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
